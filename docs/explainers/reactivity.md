## orca.fukui

### What it does
Says, atom by atom, where a molecule's electrons come from and go to when one is taken away or added, and so which atoms an attacking electrophile, nucleophile or radical is likely to meet. Four numbers per atom: **f+** (an electron added, the nucleophilic-attack function), **f-** (an electron removed, the electrophilic-attack function), **f0** (their mean, the radical-attack function) and the **dual descriptor** f+ - f-. It runs ORCA once, as three single points in one job (the neutral molecule, its cation and its anion), and compares an atom's charge in each. **A species that is itself an anion** is run the same way, its third state being a **dianion**, which no basis set binds in the gas phase: the state a calculation reaches is the basis set's and not the molecule's, so an anion's f+, f0 and dual descriptor describe the basis set at least as much as the molecule and are the least trustworthy numbers here, and the result says so with the energy by which the dianion lies above the anion. f- (where an electron comes from, the function that describes an anion) needs only the anion and the neutral radical it becomes, and the dianion is run last, so a dianion that does not converge, or whose Hirshfeld table does not sum to its charge, costs f+, f0 and the dual descriptor and nothing else: the result is f- alone and says why. Settings > Quantum chemistry can leave the dianion out altogether (on by default), which runs the anion and its neutral radical only and says so; it changes nothing for a neutral molecule or a cation. **The cation's own state is searched for** (Fukui v3): a cation can have several states a few tenths of an eV apart, one for each orbital the electron may have left, so the run also starts the cation from the neutral's orbitals with the electron taken from the HOMO-1, HOMO-2 and HOMO-3 (three more ORCA jobs), reads f- from the lowest state any of them reached (the average of the states, if the lowest level is degenerate), and says when another state lies within 0.5 eV and how far its f- is. Settings > Quantum chemistry can switch the search off, which runs the one cation job as before and says so.

### How it works
A condensed Fukui function is a finite difference of atomic populations between two states of the molecule at one geometry: f+ is an atom's population in the N+1-electron state minus its population in the N-electron state, f- is its population in the N-electron state minus the N-1 state, and f0 is half the N+1 minus the N-1 [source:olah2002] [source:thanikaivelan2002]. Written with atomic charges, which are the nuclear charge minus the population, each sign flips: this application's f+ is q(N) - q(N+1). All three are populations of the same atom in different states, so each of f+ and f- sums to 1 over the atoms.

Shipped: three single points in one ORCA input, in the order neutral, cation, anion (for a species that is itself an anion: the anion, its neutral radical, then its dianion), **all at the geometry as supplied** (the ions are not relaxed, so the numbers are vertical; optimize first if the drawn geometry is not good enough). The method and basis are the panel's. The species must be a closed shell (multiplicity 1): the cation and anion of an open-shell molecule each have more than one spin state, and nothing in the structure says which was meant, so it is refused. A charged closed-shell species is allowed; its ions are doublets. **Every job uses ORCA's `IntAcc 6.0` integration grid and states whether it is restricted** (`HFTyp RHF` for a singlet, `UHF` for a doublet): Hirshfeld charges are integrals on that grid, and ORCA keeps the unrestricted setting of the first doublet for a later closed-shell job of the same input. **At a basis set with diffuse functions** (6-31+G*, 6-311++G**, aug-cc-pVDZ, def2-SVPD and the like) each anionic state is also started from the HCore and the Hueckel guess, after its default job (an anion's own state before its dianion, which is last), and the lowest solution is read unless ORCA's default start is within 0.01 eV of it, because an anionic state there can have more than one SCF solution and ORCA's default start (the previous job's orbitals) reaches whichever the path to it favours; the result says when another start was lower. The output is read job by job: each job echoes its own charge and multiplicity, a table is assigned to a state by the charge its own job echoed and only for the molecule's own atoms, and a state whose atomic charges do not sum to its charge is refused, because that is the signature of one state's table read as another's.

**The search for other states of the cation** (`benchmarks/fukui_v3/DESIGN.md`). ORCA's default N-1 job starts from the neutral's orbitals, empties the HOMO and lets the SCF relax into whichever state is nearest, and for benzaldehyde that was not a function of the structure: 6 of 14 runs reached one state and 8 another, 0.145 eV higher and 0.13 away in f- at the oxygen. A hole start is a further cation job that reads the neutral's orbitals (`MORead` from the first job's `.gbw`) and swaps the HOMO's beta orbital with the HOMO-1's, HOMO-2's or HOMO-3's before the SCF (`%scf Rotate {a, b, 90, 1, 1} end`, the probe's procedure), so that a different state is started from. The jobs that reached one state (within 0.01 eV and 0.005 in every atom's charge, gaps measured on 44 probe jobs: the same state agreed to 0.0004, the closest two different states were 0.018 eV and 0.085 apart) are one state, f- is read from the lowest, states within 0.01 eV of it that differ in charge (benzene's two e1g components differ by up to 0.10 in one atom's charge, and their mean is uniform over the six carbons to 0.001) are averaged, and each state within 0.5 eV is reported with how far its f- is and at which atom. The holes follow the N+1 state of a neutral species or a cation, and precede the dianion of an anion, because ORCA ends a compound input at the first job that does not converge; a molecule with an element heavier than krypton (the HOMO's number cannot then be read off the electron count) or with no valence orbital below its HOMO gets no search and says so. When the occupied orbital below the deepest hole lies within 0.6 eV of the HOMO the result says a state with the hole there may exist and was not looked for. **Measured in a pilot, not a gate:** benzaldehyde on one geometry, four rotations of it and four copies displaced by 0.001 A, through real ORCA: without the search 5 of the 8 perturbed runs gave the other state's f- (0.127 to 0.130 away), with it the worst was 0.0021. The note about a competing state appears only when some start ended in that state (in the reference and in three of the four rotations, in none of the displaced geometries, where every start relaxed into the lowest state), so **the absence of a note is not evidence that no other state exists**.

**Checked against the literature, once (`benchmarks/fukui_gate_4/PREREGISTRATION.md`, outcome PARTIAL).** No earlier check used a reference in which the cation states are identified. This one read the *kind* of every state the search finds from ORCA's own spin populations of a planar molecule (a pi hole leaves its unpaired electron out of the molecular plane and a sigma hole in it: 1.000 against 0.000 for every state measured) and compared it with the states the photoelectron literature assigns. Calibrated on four molecules seen before it, pyridine's four lowest states come out in the kind and order of the highest-level calculation, with the gaps within 0.2 eV [source:trofimov2017], and thiophene's lowest state is the a2 hole with the b1 hole 0.449 eV above it, where the sources print 0.3 and 0.5 eV [source:trofimov2008]. On seven molecules never run before, the lowest state f- is read from was the kind the sources assign as lowest in all six where they fix it (pyrimidine, pyrazine and pyridazine sigma [source:gleiter1972]; imidazole, pyrazole and oxazole pi [source:patanen2021]), the second state of a diazine was the pi hole in all three, selenophene's two near-degenerate pi states came out 0.18 eV apart with the node and the weight on the selenium where they are expected [source:schafer1973], and the gap to the other kind was within 0.25 eV of the printed range in six of seven. **The one miss is pyrazole's, 1.157 eV against a single 1973 table's 1.55**, and that reference is contested in its order: two papers of 1970 assign the 10.0 eV band to the lone pair and not to a second pi state, a gap near 0.6, so the miss says little about the calculation. **Two limits it found.** The four uncontested gaps that fall outside their printed range all fall on the side where the sigma state is too low relative to the pi states (by 0.04 to 0.11 eV; pyridine in the calibration is the one comparison the other way, by 0.03 eV); a larger basis moves those gaps by 0.00 to 0.08 eV only, and a second, range-separated functional by 0.04 to 0.35 eV, in the same direction, bringing two of the four inside their ranges and taking pyrazine's out, so the size of the gap is a property of the functional and not a fixed error, while the kind of every state and their order are the same at both functionals. And the search cannot hold two states of one symmetry: in the three Cs azoles seven of eight jobs relaxed into the lowest pi state, so a close second pi state was not found, and **the note about a competing state cannot report a close state of the lowest state's own symmetry**, which the literature states too: only the lowest state of each symmetry can be treated within a delta-SCF calculation [source:holland2017]. It does not say that f- is right.

**The charge scheme is Hirshfeld's, and only Hirshfeld's.** In it the electron density at every point is divided among the atoms in proportion to the densities of the free neutral atoms there. Those weights come from neutral atoms whatever the electron count of the molecule, so the population difference is exactly the response of the atom's share of the density, not a mixture of two different partitions [source:bultinck2007]. Where the weights themselves move with the electron count (Hirshfeld-I, Bader) the two forms differ, and that paper reports about 20% of the Hirshfeld-I population-difference values negative against about 2% for the other form [source:bultinck2007]. Measured here, ORCA prints five schemes for the same job and changing only the basis from def2-SVP to def2-SVPD moved the largest per-atom f+ by 0.011 to 0.023 for Hirshfeld, 0.067 to 0.107 for Loewdin, 0.106 to 0.222 for Mulliken, 0.051 to 0.210 for MBIS and 0.042 to 0.279 for CHELPG; Mulliken put 0.23 on each hydrogen of formaldehyde and about as much on the oxygen as on the carbon, where Hirshfeld puts 0.38 to 0.40 on the carbon; MBIS and CHELPG gave the carbon a negative f-. That Hirshfeld partitioning gives non-negative values where other schemes do not was reported before [source:roy1999].

### What the result means
**Not validated, and every result says so.** The calculation failed two preregistered checks against published Hirshfeld values. In the first, of an earlier version, fifty-two of the 54 published numbers were reproduced to within 0.02 (at B3LYP/6-31G** all 23, to within 0.0066; at HF/6-31G** 29 of 31, to within 0.0101), and all 13 documented reactive-site statements were reproduced. The two that were not are one quantity, the carbon and oxygen f- of carbon monoxide at HF/6-31G**, which came out 0.0214 from the published value [source:olah2002]. Under the rule fixed beforehand that is a failure by 0.0014, and it stands. Afterwards the result was found not to depend on the integration grid, on the SCF starting guess or on the starting embedding: for the 14 jobs of the check a finer grid changed no value, no start found an anion solution lower than the default, and three embeddings agreed to 0.0004, so it is not an accident of one run. One candidate for the miss is spin contamination: the published calculations used a spin-annihilated density [source:olah2002], ORCA's unrestricted cation of carbon monoxide has an expectation value of S squared of 0.967 where a pure doublet has 0.75, and the published carbon f- (0.6931) lies between ORCA's unrestricted (0.6717) and restricted open-shell (0.7102) values. It is a candidate and not an established cause: the cations of CN-, CO2 and NCO- are as contaminated or more (1.12, 0.93 and 0.89), CO2 and NCO- agree with the published values either way, and the restricted open-shell cation of CNO- is another electronic state altogether. It is offered because it is useful and its limits are known, hidden from the default list and marked experimental. Each result repeats that it is not validated, and names an ion whose S squared is more than 10% over the doublet's 0.75.

The calculation has changed since that check, and what changed is recorded in `benchmarks/fukui_gate_2/DESIGN.md`: a species that is itself an anion was given f- alone, because its f+ comes from a dianion (the first check compared that of CN-, NCO- and CNO-, and the second decided f- alone for an anion), and the dianion was put back after that check, as the last job, because the f+ of an anion was wanted; the finer grid is on, every job states its restricted or unrestricted form, and an anionic state at a diffuse basis is started from three guesses. None of the last three moved a number at the levels the check was decided on (a finer grid: 0.0000 on its 14 jobs, at most 0.00001 on aspirin, caffeine and ibuprofen at B3LYP/6-31G*). **A second preregistered check, of the calculation as it now stands, failed too.** Its reference is the published tables of [source:deproft2002], with a second printing of the rings in [source:sablon2007]: 47 numbers and 11 reactive-site statements for 15 molecules, at B3LYP/6-31G* (6-31+G* for the two anions), on the geometry ORCA optimized at that level. Thirty-three of the numbers were within 0.02 (31 within 0.01, and 23 within 0.002) and nine of the 11 sites were reproduced. Eight molecules were reproduced in every number, to 0.0067 or better (phenol, aniline, fluorobenzene, styrene, nitrobenzene, acrolein, nitrous oxide and the nitrite ion), and seven were not: hydrogen cyanide, the sulfur of the thiocyanate ion (0.0243 off), anilinium, benzaldehyde, pyrrole, furan and thiophene, by up to 0.12. The same six neutral and cation molecules miss at the default def2-SVP preset, whose values differ from the 6-31G* ones by 0.0006 at the median and 0.0047 at most, so the basis set is not the reason. A probe made afterwards, with the published numbers in view and looking among a few cation states, found that for anilinium, benzaldehyde and thiophene the published f- values match, to 0.002 or better, a cation state that lies 0.09, 0.15 and 0.45 eV above the lowest one this calculation reaches; for benzaldehyde, the application's own input reached that higher state in 6 of 14 runs and the lowest in the other 8, the outcome changing with a rotation of one geometry, a displacement of 0.001 A and the embedding the geometry was optimized from. That is a candidate cause and not an established one, and it does not explain hydrogen cyanide, pyrrole, furan or the thiocyanate sulfur (`benchmarks/fukui_gate_2/PREREGISTRATION.md` section 11).

**A third preregistered check, of the search for other cation states, failed on one run of 114.** It asked of 12 molecules at B3LYP/6-31G* (benzaldehyde, anilinium, thiophene, hydrogen cyanide, phenol, aniline, pyrrole, furan and four held out: pyridine, toluene, naphthalene, acetophenone) whether f- is reproducible, whether the search finds every state within 0.5 eV that an exhaustive search (holes down to the HOMO-7) finds, and whether it loses a number the second check's calculation reproduced (`benchmarks/fukui_v3/PREREGISTRATION.md`). **The search was complete** on all 12 and **lost none of the 27 numbers** of the eight molecules the second check reproduced (all within 0.0067 of the published values; 22 within 0.002). **f- was reproducible in 113 of the 114 perturbed runs** (four rigid rotations and four displacements of 0.001 A of every molecule, and three re-embeddings of six), to 0.0057 or better, where the same perturbations of benzaldehyde without the search had moved it by 0.13 in 5 of 8; **the one run that was not was anilinium re-embedded, 0.0133 against the limit of 0.01, which is a different rotamer of the NH3+ group and not a different cation state** (run with the search off, the same two geometries differ by the same 0.0133). Under the rule fixed beforehand that is a failure, and it stands. The check also found that for acetophenone, as for benzaldehyde, the single cation job a run had before the search is a draw: it was on a state 0.092 eV above the lowest in 5 of 12 runs (benzaldehyde in 3 of 12, 0.146 eV), and in none of the other ten molecules. The search roughly multiplies the time of a Fukui run by 2.5, since the three hole jobs are unrestricted cations like the existing one. It does not say that the lowest state is the state a condensed Fukui function ought to describe, and it does not make the numbers match the published ones the second check missed.

One dimensionless number per atom for each function. They are descriptors of a particular partition of a particular calculation, not observables: the paper that compares the definitions concludes that different procedures give different values and that the choices an atom-condensed value needs in practice (which partition, which definition) are somewhat arbitrary [source:bultinck2007]. Compare atoms within one molecule at one method; do not compare numbers across schemes or levels of theory.

**f+ is the less trustworthy half.** A neutral closed-shell molecule has a negative electron affinity in a basis set without diffuse functions (all six cases measured here, from -1.06 to -3.02 eV), so its anion is a state the basis set only describes. The result says so, with the energy by which the anion lies above the neutral, whenever it is the case. Hirshfeld's f+ moved by at most 0.023 per atom when diffuse functions were added to the three molecules measured here, and its f- by at most 0.009, so the unbound anion is reported and not refused. The authors of the largest published table of Hirshfeld values say the same of f- against f+: generally steadier across basis sets and levels of theory [source:olah2002].

**Diffuse functions are not a cure for it, and f+ with them is not reproducible.** In the gate's reported arm (shown beside the outcome and never decided on), at 6-311+G** on the seven species of the published table, f- agreed with the published values in all 17 rows (largest difference 0.012) and f+ in 6 of 17 (median difference 0.10 per atom at B3LYP and 0.20 at HF, up to 0.33) [source:olah2002]. Probes made afterwards, in a few cases only, found two separate things behind it. Seven of the 14 jobs at that basis were refused, because the anion's Hirshfeld charges summed between 0.010 and 0.073 short of its charge (carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and the cyanate ion at both levels, the fulminate ion at HF); that is ORCA's default integration grid missing part of a diffuse electron, and on a finer grid every anion came within 0.005 electrons (at 6-31G** a finer grid changed no value). And the unbound anion has more than one SCF solution at that basis: of six cases probed, three had a solution 0.6 to 1.0 eV below the one ORCA reaches by default, with f+ differing by up to 0.31 between them, and the published values match the lower solution for acetone at HF and the higher one for CN- at HF. For the fulminate and carbon dioxide anions at B3LYP/6-311+G**, one molecule embedded three ways gave two different anions (f+ on the carbon 0.85 against 0.63, and 0.71 against 0.59) where f- agreed to 0.0001. At the 6-31G** levels the check was decided on none of this happened: the default solution was the lowest found in all 14 cases and all 14 jobs reproduced across three embeddings to 0.0004. So do not add diffuse functions to improve f+. The finer grid is now on by default (it changed no value at the levels the check was decided on, at most 0.0004 in the f- of seven bound anions at B3LYP/6-31+G*, and costs 8% to 28% of a run on three drug-sized molecules), and at a basis set with diffuse functions an anionic state is started from three guesses and the lowest solution is read, with a sentence in the result when another start was lower: on acetone at HF/6-311+G** that is the solution 1.01 eV below ORCA's default one, whose f+ puts 0.06 on the oxygen where the default one puts 0.25. f+ at such a basis is still not validated, and a state with several solutions has no single well-defined value.

A negative value is the partition compensating, not an atom that loses density on gaining an electron; it is reported as it came out and flagged. No Hirshfeld f+ or f- was negative in any of the six runs measured here (three molecules, two basis sets).

### When to use it, when not
Use it to rank the atoms of one molecule as sites for a nucleophile, an electrophile or a radical, and to see how a substituent moves that ranking at one method. Where a molecule's cation has a second state close to its lowest (anilinium 0.09 eV, benzaldehyde 0.15 and thiophene 0.45 above it were the cases measured, and a published table agreed with the higher one), the f- you get is that of the LOWEST state the search found, which is not the state the published values describe, and the result names the others it found within 0.5 eV with how far their f- is; read the ranking of such a molecule's atoms as one of two, and remember that the note appears only when a start ended in the other state. With the search switched off in Settings it is the f- of whichever state ORCA's SCF reached from the neutral's orbitals, which for benzaldehyde changed with a rotation of the same geometry, a displacement of 0.001 A and the embedding it was optimized from. For an anion read f- first: it is the question one asks of an anion (which atom an electrophile meets), it needs only the anion and the neutral radical, and the f+ of an anion comes from a dianion that no basis set binds. Measured here: methoxide at B3LYP/6-31+G* has its dianion 5.90 eV above the anion, with the extra electron on the three hydrogens (on an RDKit geometry their Hirshfeld charge is -0.32 each against -0.07 in the anion), so f+ is largest on a hydrogen, 0.24; cyanide at B3LYP/6-31G** has it 13.03 eV above, and its f+ (0.595 on carbon, 0.405 on nitrogen) is the first check's stored value for it to 0.00001, a value that check had matched to the published ones within 0.0043. The second check's two anions at B3LYP/6-31+G* also converge, with dianions 4.83 eV (thiocyanate) and 7.37 eV (nitrite) above them, f+ largest on sulfur (0.52) and on nitrogen (0.42), and f- unchanged. The authors of a method for computing Fukui functions without finite differences say of the finite-difference f+ that it is hard or impossible for the many molecules whose anion is metastable [source:sablon2007]. Lean on f- more than on f+, which is the half that moved with the basis set, in the published table as well as here [source:olah2002]. A 2019 benchmark found the Fukui function the weaker descriptor, and weakest for nucleophiles. On seven molecules with experimentally established reaction sites, the Hirshfeld charge itself gave the site in all seven, while the Fukui function from none of 15 charge schemes did; with Hirshfeld charges it matched the established site for three of the four electrophilic attacks and one of the three nucleophilic ones (read from its Tables 1 and 2). Over 43 systems with experimental scales the Hirshfeld charge correlated with electrophilicity at R = 0.995 and with nucleophilicity at 0.988, the Fukui function at 0.905 and 0.344 [source:wang2019]. That study used M06-2X/6-311+G(d) with an implicit dichloromethane solvent, so it measures the descriptors and not this application's default level; it is the reason f+ and f- here are offered as a ranking of atoms within one molecule, not as a predictor of where a reaction goes.

Do not treat the largest value as a prediction of where a reaction goes: the authors of the largest published table of Hirshfeld values report that the atom with the largest value matched the experimentally known site in every case they tested, while noting that their own anion results were not predicted satisfactorily [source:olah2002], and the same scheme failed to pick the carbonyl carbon of acetaldehyde and acetone in another study [source:thanikaivelan2002]. Do not average it over conformers (it is computed for one geometry, and the conformer-averaging option is refused for it). An open-shell species is refused.
